Revenue operating system · pre-construction real estate · 2020-21

From first touch to transaction: the system that stopped agents chasing.

The human here is a pre-construction agent working on commission. A lead used to arrive from a form at nine at night and get a phone call on Thursday, by which time the buyer had already spoken to two other people. What changed is that every lead got an answer within a minute, got asked the same questions in the same order, and landed on his phone with the answers attached and a stage already set in the CRM. He stopped chasing and started picking.

01

What it is

Pre-construction real estate had a structural gap in it. Project-marketing firms sold to developers, and the developer's marketing ended at the sales centre door. The individual agent bringing buyers to those projects had ad spend, a phone and a spreadsheet.

So the product was never a single tool. It was one operating system covering the whole distance from the first ad impression to a signed contract, with the CRM as the spine rather than a filing cabinet at the end of it.

Six stages, each with an owner and a definition of done. Acquire brought demand in through paid media and agent-branded project sites, so the lead belonged to the agent. Capture pulled every source into one flow. Prequalify asked a fixed set of questions before anybody spent an hour on a coffee. Nurture held the thread across a buying cycle measured in months. Route handed the agent a person with context. Close and measure fed the result back into the next round of targeting.

The part people underrate is Route. Most funnels break at the handoff, where a scored lead becomes a name on a list and the context evaporates. Here the handoff carried the transcript, the qualification answers and the reason the system thought this person was ready, and it moved the CRM stage at the same time so nothing depended on the agent remembering.

CRM orchestration

Everything above ran on one CRM instance per agent team. Five mechanisms did the work.

MechanismWhat it did
TaggingEvery intent the conversation detected became a tag, and tags were the vocabulary the rest of the system read.
Stage movementA qualified answer moved the record automatically, so the pipeline reflected what buyers did and nobody had to remember to update it.
Campaign triggersSequences started and stopped on tags and stages, which meant a person who booked stopped being nurtured for the thing they had already done.
Notification and routingOwnership rules decided who got the alert, and the alert carried the context with it.
AttributionSource held all the way through to transaction, so ad spend could be judged on closings, which is the only number that pays anybody.

The conversation layer at the Capture and Prequalify stages went through three technology generations, starting with rule-based workflows years before generative AI. That story, with the screenshots, is on the Side.io page.

My role

I defined the system that filled the gap. The business model and the customer journey first, then the funnel logic, the qualification criteria, the CRM architecture and the handoff contract. I set what each stage had to produce before a record could move to the next one.

I directed implementation and ran the iteration on conversion, which is where most of the real work was. I reviewed what shipped against the stage definitions. I also converted the whole thing from a services engagement into something an agent could buy on a subscription, which changed what we had to build. A service can be held together by an operator paying attention. A product cannot.

What I did not do. I did not build the machine-learning models that later replaced the rule-based conversation layer. Our CTO and a small engineering team built those, and a University of British Columbia master's capstone team contributed a later piece. I set the requirements, chose when to make each transition and led the team through it. If you ask me to explain the classifier architecture I will tell you what it was for and what it cost us. If you ask me to derive it, I will tell you that was somebody else's chair.

Where these numbers come from

1% to 3% is lead-to-transaction conversion. The first figure is what the funnel held under the rule-based generation, which was roughly the industry baseline, and the second is where it landed once the in-house models were carrying the qualification. Both come from the company's own reporting for that period and appear again in the technical narratives we filed for federal funding programs.

~$45M is year-one transaction value across the projects the system carried. It is the value of the transactions themselves. It was never company revenue, and I would rather label it plainly here than have somebody assume otherwise and then discount everything else I said.

1,000+ buyer conversations a day is the volume the conversation layer handled at its peak, counted in the CRM.

The honest caveat: these are internal figures with internal reporting and funding filings behind them. There was no independent audit of the funnel.
