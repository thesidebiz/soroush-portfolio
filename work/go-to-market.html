How I take a product to market.

Twice I have built a business by selling to people the market had skipped: the pre-construction agent working on commission with ad spend, a phone and a spreadsheet, and the studio that had to feed a crew on repeat. Nobody was serving them, and each had a budget and a reason to come back. Both times the work ran the same way. I sat with the customer until I knew how they decided, shaped the offer around what they stood to lose, built the machine from the first ad to the closed deal, and owned the number at the end of it. 01

Side: selling to the agent nobody served

Pre-construction real estate, 2020-21, before generative AI. The customer was the agent; the agent's customer was the homebuyer.

The segment. Project-marketing firms sold to developers. Nobody was doing that job for the individual agent who brought buyers to those projects. He made videos and posted on social media hoping a buyer would turn up, and he worked on commission with ad spend, a phone and a spreadsheet. I saw the gap. He was the customer.

Getting close. I moved into a brokerage to sit next to the agents, learn their pains and their processes, and take their feedback every day. Three things came out of that. They wanted close to no risk, which meant a commission split and no fixed fee. They did not trust online leads. And they did not count an online lead as a client; a client was someone they had met.

The offer. So we stopped selling leads and took the whole job: project selection, the online ads, lead capture, prequalification, nurture, appointment setting and the follow-ups after a showroom visit, all run by us, with customer-care callers doing the heavy lifting on the phone. Every presale project got a site under the agent's own name, so the lead belonged to him. A buyer asking at eleven at night whether the parking stall was included got an answer in seconds, out of a project database. What the agent received was an appointment with a qualified buyer, and the closing. His other options were a project-marketing firm that answered to the developer, or online leads he did not trust. Side was the third option: his own name on the site, a qualified buyer already in his calendar, and nothing owed until a deal closed.

Selling to the agents. The sale happened inside the brokerage, face to face, on an offer with nothing to lose. The first closings were the pitch, and after that the pitch was not mine. An agent who walked out of an appointment we set with a signed buyer did the selling to the next agent, and adoption spread desk by desk. That is the signal I wait for before I believe a market is there: the selling going on while I am out of the room. The brokerage had more than 80 agents. Fifteen of them ran the full offer with us, and the brokerage used the same system to feed leads to the rest.

The buyer channels. Paid social and search into agent-branded project sites. SMS and email sequencing to hold the thread across a cycle measured in months. One CRM instance per agent team carried the tags, the stage movement, the campaign triggers, the routing and the attribution.

The pricing. A commission split, paid on the closed deal and nothing before it, because that is what agents would sign. Today that would be called outcome-based pricing. Then it was the only deal an agent would take. Our share started at 30 percent and moved to 50 percent for the same job, once the results had held long enough to argue from. Then we hired our own agents to work the appointments and own the closing as well, at 70 percent, which made Side the agent as well as the system behind him. A subscription sat alongside the split as a second way to buy the same service.

The feedback loop. The handoff, the follow-ups after a showroom visit and the way a cold lead was brought back were each rebuilt from what the agents told us at those desks, more than once.

The result. The number the agents judged us on was lead-to-transaction conversion, and it moved from 1% to 3%. The conversation layer behind it handled 1,000+ buyer conversations a day at its peak, counted in the CRM. Year-one transaction value across the projects the system carried was about $45M, which is the value of the transactions themselves.

Lazymeal: a marketplace from zero, then a new segment

Food marketplace, 2012 to 2020. Home delivery first, then corporate catering.

The first market. Lazymeal started in 2012 as home delivery, when Just Eat was the only other name in the city. Being early in an underserved city is a real advantage and it does not last. We grew several times over each year while it did. Both sides of the marketplace were the same week's work: selling to the people who ordered, onboarding vendors one kitchen at a time, and keeping order volume ahead of supply so the marketplace stayed liquid for both sides.

The shift. Then Foodora, DoorDash and Uber Eats arrived with budgets no local marketplace could match, and a consumer business without money like that was going to lose. The answer was to move the segment before the numbers forced us to. In 2015 and 2016 I took that on myself, studied how food businesses elsewhere had answered the same problem, and went looking for customers with purchasing power and repeat orders. I found them in corporate catering, where the competitor already there, Foodee, was doing one-off orders. The gap was the office that fed its people on repeat: a studio crew on overtime, an office admin collecting everyone's order and placing it one restaurant at a time.

The lighthouse. Film production companies and game studios fitted that gap, and we landed Microsoft's Xbox game studio in Vancouver. I went back and forth between their office and ours until I understood how they ordered, we built a virtual cafeteria around it, and then we made that the product we sold to the next studio.

The sales. With one account working I had a reference. I ran the business-to-business sales myself, took that reference into every studio in town that fed a crew the same way, and closed every one of these accounts: Sony Pictures Imageworks, EA, Scanline VFX, ICON Creative Studio, Mainframe Studios, Animal Logic, FuseFX, Industrial Light & Magic and Image Engine, among others. Each one was a repeat-order customer.

The result. $17M+ in gross food sales from 2012 to 2020, counted as marketplace GMV, across 400+ vendor kitchens.

800 diagnoses, and the systems I run now

The diagnoses. As a government-appointed advisor under the Canada Digital Adoption Program I led the practice that assessed 800 businesses. That is 800 readings of where a business is stuck, and it is almost never where the owner says it is. I listen to why they did what they did, I ask the hard questions, and then I write the roadmap. The practice delivered 300 roadmaps, each one sequenced by what the business could staff. Anything that needed a person they did not have and were not going to hire came out of the plan.

The systems. I architect and run the client-acquisition systems for ~45 active clients: demand generation, landing pages, qualification, nurture, CRM orchestration, handoff and attribution, on one platform. Source has to hold all the way through to the closed transaction, because that is the only number anyone gets paid on, so it is designed into the data model at the start. On a client account today the click identifiers, the campaign and ad ids, the full set of tracking parameters and the lifetime purchase total sit on the same contact record, and a workflow updates the return per contact from them.

What travels. Running the same system across that many businesses is how I know which parts are the product and which were only ever true of one client. The parts that travel are the ones with no single client's judgement inside them: the contact data model with attribution on it, the capture and booking flows, the handoff, the reporting. The parts rebuilt every time are the offer, the qualifying questions and the routing rules, because those are the client's judgement about their own customer.

Since then. The channel that has moved most is search itself, and the engine on this site sweeps positioning queries against an answer engine on every run. Nightshift has the detail. What I want next is one product to own, from the build to the market.

The method, in four steps

The same four steps whether the thing being sold is software, a marketplace or a service somebody buys on repeat. They came out of the two businesses above, in this order, and each one has a test for when it is done.

1. The segment, from the inside

I start with who is underserved, and I go and sit with them: a desk inside the brokerage, months of going back and forth with one studio before the first standing order. I would rather hold most of a narrow segment than a sliver of a broad one. Done when I can say, in the customer's own words, what they are afraid of losing and what they already count as value.

2. The offer, shaped to what the customer risks

The agents would not carry a fee and would not trust an online lead, and they counted value in closed deals. So the offer was no fixed fee and no leads: appointments that showed up and deals that closed, priced on the deal. I pick the number the paying customer uses to judge us and make everyone read that one, because it is also the number that says when to change course. At Side it was lead-to-transaction conversion. Done when the first customers sign without an argument.

3. The whole job, and the system under it

The agents' value sat at the end of a long chain, so we took the whole chain, including the parts that needed a person, and put one system under it with the CRM as the spine. I specify the handoff before anything else: what the salesperson receives, what moves in the CRM at the same moment, and what happens when nobody picks it up. At Side the handoff carried the transcript, the buyer's qualification answers and the reason the system thought this person was ready, and it moved the CRM stage at the same time. Done when source holds from the first click to the closed transaction.

4. A price that moves with the results

A success-based price is a promise you get to renegotiate every time you keep it. Side's share went from 30 to 50 percent doing the same job, once the results had held long enough to argue from. The move to 70 was a larger job at a larger share, with our own agents working the appointments and taking the close. Only the first move is evidence of pricing power, and it is the one I plan for. Then I turn what worked into something a buyer can buy again. Done when the second customer buys what the first one taught us to build.
